Where the numbers come from

Astrodoc uses two kinds of data. The mission's radiation, the limits and the medical rules come from NASA measurements, NASA standards and published astronaut studies. The three crew members' readings (heart rate, sleep, blood tests) are simulated, because real astronaut health records are private. Every rule below says which is which.

Radiation

NASA's Curiosity rover carried a radiation detector, RAD, inside its spacecraft on the way to Mars and then on the planet's surface. Astrodoc uses those measured dose rates for every day of the mission.

Inside a spacecraft in deep space
1.8 mSv a dayZeitlin et al., Science, 2013. Measured during Curiosity's 253-day cruise.
On the surface of Mars
0.67 mSv a dayHassler et al., Science, 2014. Gale Crater, August 2012 to June 2013.
NASA career limit
600 mSvNASA-STD-3001 Vol. 1 Rev. B. The same for every age and sex.
Solar storm shelter
under 250 mSv per stormNASA-STD-3001: the spacecraft's shelter must keep one large solar particle event below this.
Natural background on Earth
about 2.4 mSv a yearUNSCEAR world average. One day in deep space is about 9 months of it.

What Astrodoc does with it. The mission has three phases: 253 days to Mars (Curiosity's real cruise time), 500 days on Mars (NASA's reference plan) and 253 days back. Adding the measured rates day by day gives about 1,250 mSv for the whole mission, roughly twice the career limit. The dashboard shows each person's line, the day they pass 600 mSv and the total at the end. Amber starts at half the limit (300 mSv) and red above 480 mSv: our team's proposal.

Blood clots

On the International Space Station, ultrasound of 11 crew members found slow or reversed blood flow in the neck vein (internal jugular) in 6 of them around flight day 50, and a blood clot in one. It was found by a research ultrasound, not by symptoms. That is why Astrodoc watches blood tests, not only symptoms.

Crew with stagnant or reversed neck-vein flow
6 of 11Marshall-Fazio et al., JAMA Network Open, 2019.
Clot found
1 occlusive clotSame study. NASA's 2025 venous thromboembolism working group report covers this risk.

What Astrodoc does with it. D-dimer above 0.5 mg/L FEU is amber. D-dimer rising while platelets fall below 150 and fibrinogen below 200 is red: “possible blood clot”. A D-dimer that rises over three tests is reported to the flight surgeon automatically. The lab ranges are standard adult reference ranges chosen by our medical students; a flight surgeon must confirm them.

Sleep

A ten-year study of 85 astronauts found they slept about 6 hours a night in space (5.96 hours on shuttle missions, 6.09 on the ISS), although NASA scheduled 8.5. Three quarters of ISS crew members took sleep medication at some point.

Average sleep in space
about 6 hoursBarger et al., The Lancet Neurology, 2014.

What Astrodoc does with it. Less than 7 hours is amber; less than 6 hours, or 17 hours awake, is red. Short sleep, slower reactions and a high heart rate together mean “very tired”, because tired people rarely notice it themselves. Thresholds: our team's proposal.

Eyes and vision

On long missions fluid shifts toward the head and can change the eye and vision (Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome, SANS). Astrodoc asks about vision in “Check how I feel” and suggests an onboard eye check. Source: NASA-STD-3001 technical brief OCHMO-MTB-001.

Earth is minutes away

Depending on where the planets are, a message to Mars takes about 3 to 22 minutes one way. The demo uses 18 minutes, so a reply takes at least 36. That is why Astrodoc never waits for Earth: it says what to do now, sends the report by itself and shows when the earliest reply can arrive.

About every 26 months Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from Earth (solar conjunction), and NASA stops sending commands to Mars missions for about two weeks. Astrodoc keeps working with no link at all: the health rules run on board, and reports wait and go out by themselves when the link returns. Turn on “Earth link down” in the demo controls to see it.

The last 6 months

Real astronauts' day-by-day health records are private, so NASA does not publish them. NASA does publish what it measured across many astronauts, and the dashboard's 6-month history follows those results week by week for each simulated crew member:

Sleep on the ISS
6.09 h a nightAverage of ISS crews, against 8.5 h scheduled. Barger et al., The Lancet Neurology, 2014.
Bone density
1–1.5% lost a monthOn 4–6 month missions. NASA Human Research Program.
Body mass
7% lost in a yearScott Kelly, who also ate about 30% fewer calories than expected. NASA Twins Study, 2019.

What Astrodoc does with it. For each person it compares the last 4 weeks with the first 4, and their numbers with NASA's: sleep under 6 hours is red and under 7 amber; bone loss faster than 1.5% a month is amber; body mass down more than 5% is amber; a falling mood is amber. The analysis says which of these need attention and which are on track.

Astrodoc on Earth

The problem Astrodoc solves, help that is too far away to ask, is not only a space problem. In 2021 about 4.5 billion people, more than half the world, were not fully covered by essential health services (WHO and World Bank, 2023).

Concordia station, Antarctica
8 months, nothing in or outCrews stay at least eight months, four of them without sunlight; in winter nothing can reach or leave the base, and one doctor looks after everyone. ESA uses it to study life on a spaceship. ESA
People without full essential health services
about 4.5 billion2021 figure. WHO and World Bank

What carries over unchanged. The rules run on the device, so a polar station, a ship or a village clinic gets the same help as the spacecraft. It installs and works with no internet. Emergency signs come first. Reports wait and go out by themselves when a connection returns. The words are plain and the text is large for tired eyes.

What would change, honestly. Before anyone relied on it, doctors would have to check every rule for that place, and it would need the local language. For Uzbekistan that means Uzbek and Russian, checked by our medical students and a doctor. That is our next step, not something the prototype does today.

What is simulated

The commander, pilot and mission specialist are invented. Their heart rate, temperature, blood pressure, sleep, mood, reaction times and blood tests are made up to show three stories: a very tired commander, a pilot with a possible clot, and a healthy specialist. Their radiation uses the real rates above, plus a made-up dose from earlier missions. With the Arduino pulse sensor connected, the heart rate on the dashboard is a real measurement.
