        What the team found

        • Radiation builds up over a whole career, so the app tracks a running total, not a daily reading.
        • Heavy ions damage DNA densely along their path. NASA lists four risks: cancer, central nervous system effects, heart and degenerative tissue disease, and acute radiation syndrome.
        • Dose rate vs the ISS: Moon about 1.5×, deep space 2–3×, Mars surface about the same. A three-year Mars mission is about 10× current ISS exposure.
        • Water and polyethylene shield well against solar particles, not enough against cosmic rays.
        • NASA career limit: 600 mSv for everyone (NASA-STD-3001 Vol. 1 Rev. B).

          Why this matters

          In 11 ISS crew members, 6 had stagnant or reversed blood flow in the neck vein around flight day 50. One had a blocking clot, found with an ultrasound guided live by doctors on Earth. High D-dimer alone does not prove a clot; the dangerous pattern is high D-dimer + low platelets + low fibrinogen.

          Marshall-Goebel K, et al. JAMA Network Open, 2019. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.15011

          Combined rules

            Keeping it honest

            • All crew data is simulated. CDR, PLT and MS1 are roles, not real people.
            • A rested person's real heart rate is normal. In the demo, either raise it with 30 seconds of exercise before measuring, or show the value as simulated.
            • Normal in-flight ranges for clotting tests are still unknown. Earth lab ranges are used.
            • This is a prototype for a hackathon. It is not a medical device.
